Hazard reporting for cleaning staff: how to get problems reported before someone gets hurt

By Barry Williams, founder of SeeMySite, from 25 years of visiting cleaning companies · November 20, 2026

Your cleaners see things nobody else does. They are in the building when it’s empty, they walk every corridor, and they notice the loose handrail, the leaking pipe and the door that no longer locks. The question is whether any of that ever reaches you.

Picture a Tuesday night. A cleaner notices that a light in a stairwell has been out for a week, and that the bottom step is cracked. They think “someone will have told the building manager by now” and carry on. On Thursday morning somebody else walks down those stairs.

That is a hazard that was seen, and not reported. This guide is about closing that gap, and it is a plain five-step approach for managers with a small crew:

Why cleaners don’t report hazards

It is rarely because they don’t care. The usual reasons are ordinary:

  • “It’s not my job.” They were hired to clean, so they assume problems belong to someone else.
  • “Someone must have told them already.” Everybody assumes somebody else has reported it.
  • “Nothing happens when I do.” If the last report disappeared into silence, the next one won’t be made.
  • Fear of blame. If the report might sound like “I broke it” or “I wasn’t careful,” it is easier to say nothing.
  • No easy way. There is nobody to call at 10 pm, no form, and the manager doesn’t answer the phone.
  • Language. Some cleaners don’t have the words, or the confidence, to describe the problem to a customer or to you.

Notice that most of these are the manager’s to fix, not the cleaner’s.

What counts as a hazard on a cleaning site

Make sure everybody has the same list. It will vary by site, but usually includes:

  • Wet or slippery floors, and spills that are not yours
  • Damaged cords, plugs, equipment or ladders
  • Chemicals that are unlabeled, decanted into the wrong container, or stored where they shouldn’t be
  • Blocked exits or fire equipment, or broken emergency lighting
  • Broken locks, alarms, lights or stairs, and anything that affects site security
  • Needles, broken glass, bodily fluids or other biohazards
  • Unsafe access: a loose handrail, a damaged ramp, a low ceiling, an unstable floor
  • People: threats, aggression, or someone on site who shouldn’t be

The five steps

  1. Make it normalTell every new cleaner on day one that reporting a hazard is part of the job, and thank the first person who does. It belongs in your first-30-days plan: see the first 30 days for a new cleaner.
  2. Make it easyOne way to report, that works from the site, in any language the crew uses. A phone call, a text, or a message with a photo. The harder it is, the fewer reports you get.
  3. Make it safeTell cleaners plainly that reporting a hazard will never be held against them, and mean it. If the report gets someone into trouble, you will not get the next one.
  4. Act, and close the loopEvery report gets a reply, even if the reply is “thanks, we’ve told the building manager.” People keep reporting when they see something happen.
  5. Record it and look for patternsKeep a dated record of each report, what was done and when. Then look: the same site, the same fault, the same time of year.

What a good report contains

You do not need a long form. Five facts are enough, and a photo is worth most of them:

  • Where: the site and the exact spot.
  • What: what the cleaner saw, in plain words.
  • When: date and time they noticed it.
  • Who: who is at risk, and whether anyone has been hurt.
  • What they did: for example, put out a wet floor sign or taped off the area.

Make safe first, then tell

A simple rule helps: if the hazard can hurt someone in the next few hours, the cleaner makes it safe if they can do so without taking a risk themselves (a sign, a barrier, closing a door), and then tells you straight away. If they can’t make it safe, they report it first and keep people away. In either case they should never be left feeling that it is their problem to solve alone.

Tell the customer too

If the hazard belongs to the building (a broken step, a leak, a faulty alarm), the customer needs to know, and it is much better coming from you than from an incident report. A quick message with a photo and the time it was noticed shows you are on top of things, and it protects your crew if something does go wrong.

Records, and what the rules require

Your cleaners’ employer duties, and what you must record and report, depend on where you work. Check what applies in your state or country, and if you are unsure, ask your insurer or a safety adviser. Whatever the rules say, a dated record of who reported what, when, and what you did about it is the single most useful thing to have if there is ever a claim or a dispute. Written is good, and electronic and time-stamped is better.

A checklist for your crew

  1. BeforeA short, agreed policy; a list of what counts as a hazard; one easy way to report; and a promise that reporting is safe.
  2. At the siteMake it safe if you can, report straight away with a photo, and tell the customer if the problem is the building’s.
  3. AfterwardsA reply to the cleaner, a record of what was done, and a regular look for patterns across sites.
